# The Christian building at Dura-Europos

> c. 235–256 CE. Source record: Yale University Art Gallery, Dura-Europos collection.

At Dura-Europos on the Euphrates, excavators in the 1920s and 1930s uncovered a
private house that had been remodelled for Christian use. A room was adapted as a
baptistery. Its walls carry the earliest surviving painted Christian images: the good
shepherd, the healing of the paralytic, the women at the tomb, and Adam and Eve.

The building was destroyed when the city fell to the Sasanians around 256/257 CE. The
wall paintings were removed and are now in the Yale University Art Gallery, which holds
the Dura-Europos collection from the Yale–French Academy excavations.

What it establishes: Christian worship in a purpose-modified domestic building in the
235-256 window, with figurative art well before the Constantinian turn. It is a fixed
chronological anchor for early Christian iconography and architecture.

What it does not establish: the content of the community's gospel texts, which have not
survived from the site, or any direct link to Jesus's lifetime. The graffiti and
inscriptions at the site include the name "Christ" and Old Testament scenes used
typologically.

Dating rests on the city's destruction layer and on coins and inscriptions from the
occupation, not on the paintings themselves.
