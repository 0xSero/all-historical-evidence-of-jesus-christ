# The Pilate inscription (Caesarea Maritima)

> 1st century CE (stone); found 1961. Source record: Israel Museum, Jerusalem (accession 1962-118); Italian excavation at Caesarea directed by Antonio Frova.

A limestone block, reused in a later stairway at Caesarea Maritima, carries a Latin
dedicatory inscription naming **Pontius Pilate**. The surviving text reads
`[PON]TIVS PILATVS [PRAEF]ECTVS IVDA[EA]E` — "Pontius Pilate, Prefect of Judaea".

The stone was found in 1961 during excavations of the Roman theatre at Caesarea. That
excavation was directed by Antonio Frova on behalf of the Italian Archaeological Mission.
The block is now in the Israel Museum, Jerusalem.

What it establishes: Pilate's existence, his office, and his presence in Judaea in the
first century. It is the only contemporary physical monument that names him.

What it does not establish: nothing about Jesus, his trial, or the Gospel narratives of
Pilate. An inscription confirms an official and his title; it does not authenticate
conversations attributed to him in literature written decades later.

The title matters for a different reason. The Gospels call Pilate *hegemon*
(governor). The stone calls him *praefectus*. That difference fits what is otherwise
known of the equestrian prefects who governed Judaea from 6 to 41 CE, before the
province was given procurators of higher rank.
