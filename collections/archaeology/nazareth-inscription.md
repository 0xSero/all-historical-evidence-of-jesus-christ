# The Nazareth inscription (imperial edict on tomb violation)

> 1st century CE (text); stone of unknown provenance. Source record: Bibliothèque nationale de France; provenance study published 2024.

A marble slab bearing a 24-line Greek edict of an unnamed emperor orders that tombs
and grave goods not be disturbed, and prescribes the death penalty for violation. It
entered the collection of the Bibliothèque nationale de France in the 1930s, acquired
through the Italian scholar Antonio Frova, and became known as the "Nazareth
inscription" because of a reported association with Nazareth.

The text is usually assigned to the first century CE, and often to Claudius or Augustus.
Its provenance is not documented, which is the central problem.

A 2024 study by Kyle Harper and colleagues in the *Journal of Archaeological Science*
analysed strontium isotopes in the marble and argued that the stone most likely came from
a quarry on Kos, not from Judaea. If that holds, the object is a Roman imperial edict
about tomb violation that has nothing to do with Nazareth or with the empty tomb
narratives, and its appearance in Nazareth was a later accident of collection history.

What it establishes: Roman imperial interest in punishing tomb robbery, an offence
relevant to how burial and sealed tombs were policed.

What it does not establish: anything about Jesus's tomb, and, on the isotope evidence,
probably not even a Galilean findspot. The dossier cites it as a case study in how
provenance claims fail.
