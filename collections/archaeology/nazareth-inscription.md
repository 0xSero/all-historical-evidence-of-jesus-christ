# The Nazareth inscription (imperial edict on tomb violation)

> 1st century CE (text); stone of undocumented findspot. Source record: Bibliothèque nationale de France; provenance study published 2020.

A marble slab bearing a 22-line Greek edict of an unnamed emperor orders that tombs and
grave goods not be disturbed, and threatens capital punishment for violation. It opens
with "Edict of Caesar", and its date is assigned by letter forms to the first half of the
first century CE.

The stone's history is the reason it is discussed at all. Wilhelm Fröhner acquired it in
1878 and recorded it as "a marble slab sent from Nazareth in 1878". That note says where
it was sent from, not where it was found; Nazareth was an active antiquities market in the
1870s. It has been in the Bibliothèque nationale de France, in the Cabinet des Médailles,
since 1925, and Franz Cumont published it in 1930.

A study by Kyle Harper and colleagues in the *Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports*
30 (2020), article 102228, analysed strontium isotopes in the marble and argued that the
stone most likely came from a quarry on Kos, not from Judaea. If that holds, the object is
a Roman imperial edict about tomb violation that has nothing to do with Nazareth or with
the empty-tomb narratives, and its association with Nazareth reflects the shipping history
of a Paris collection rather than a findspot.

What it establishes: Roman imperial interest in punishing tomb robbery, an offence
relevant to how burial and sealed tombs were policed.

What it does not establish: anything about Jesus's tomb. It does not even establish that
the stone was found in Galilee. The dossier cites it as a case study in how provenance
claims fail, not as evidence for the resurrection.
