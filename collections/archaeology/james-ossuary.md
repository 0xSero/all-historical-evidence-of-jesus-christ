# The James ossuary (disputed)

> 1st century CE (claimed); surfaced 2002. Source record: Israel Antiquities Authority examination; unprovenanced private collection.

A limestone ossuary bearing the Aramaic inscription `Ya'akov bar Yosef akhui
diYeshua` — "James, son of Joseph, brother of Jesus" — was presented to the public in
2002. It came from a private collection and had no recorded excavation context.

The Israel Antiquities Authority convened a committee, which reported in 2003. The
committee held that parts of the inscription, and the patina, had been added in modern
times. The collector, Oded Golan, was tried in Jerusalem; the court acquitted him in 2012
of forgery charges, finding that the prosecution had not proved the case beyond
reasonable doubt. That verdict is not a finding of authenticity, and specialists remain
divided. Later patina and isotope studies have been read both ways.

What it establishes: that a first-century-style ossuary with this inscription exists, and
that the legal case against the collector failed.

What it does not establish: that the inscription is ancient as it stands, or that the
person named is James of Jerusalem. Because the object is unprovenanced, the normal
archaeological tests cannot be applied. Read it as a contested artifact, not as evidence
for the Gospels.

The phrase "brother of Jesus" is the crux. Naming a brother on an ossuary is rare, which
is exactly why the inscription matters if genuine, and why it attracted forgery
allegations if not.
