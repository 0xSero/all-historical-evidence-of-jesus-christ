# The prophecies associated with Jesus: a critical catalogue

## What this catalogue counts

This is a catalogue of 100 substantial scriptural connections, arranged approximately
in the order of Jesus's narrated life, death, resurrection, and the movement that followed
him. It includes predictions, promises, symbols, narrative patterns, and passages
subsequently interpreted as predictions. It is not a claim that the Hebrew Bible contains
exactly 100 independent forecasts of Jesus. No ancient canon supplies a numbered master
list. A list can reach 300 or more by dividing a single passage into many details, counting
parallel Gospel accounts separately, including loose similarities, and adding later
interpretations.

The distinction matters. Matthew explicitly says that Hosea 11:1 was fulfilled in Jesus's
return from Egypt, although Hosea describes Israel's past exodus. That is typology: a later
life reenacts and completes an earlier sacred pattern. It is a real ancient interpretive
practice, but it differs from a dated forecast identifying a future event. Matthew's infancy
narrative also contains an alleged prophetic statement about a Nazorean for which no
exact surviving Old Testament verse exists. These should be explained, not silently
converted into modern predictions. [P08]

Four labels are used below:

• Explicit NT application: a New Testament author quotes or unmistakably applies the
  earlier passage to Jesus or his movement. This establishes the interpretation, not
  independently the event's historicity.

• NT allusion: the wording or narrative resembles an earlier passage, but there is no
  explicit declaration that this particular passage predicted the event.

• Typology: a person, institution, ritual, or earlier event supplies a pattern interpreted
  through Jesus.

• Later Christian reading: a connection is common in subsequent Christianity or
  apologetics but is not clearly made in the New Testament.

References normally follow common English Bible numbering. Hebrew, Catholic, Orthodox,
and Septuagint editions sometimes number verses differently, especially in Psalms,
Malachi, Micah, and Jonah. The reference is therefore more important than an isolated
translated phrase. Biblical chapter-and-verse references are primary-source citations; the
numbered references identify additional retrieved editions or contextual scholarship.

## 1. Ancestry, covenant, and royal identity

**1. The woman's offspring defeats the serpent.** Genesis 3:15; compare Romans 16:20,
Galatians 4:4, Revelation 12:1-17. Later Christian reading, with NT thematic
parallels. The original scene explains hostility between the serpent and humanity.
Christian tradition calls it the protoevangelium, the first gospel. Romans associates Satan's
defeat with believers; Genesis does not explicitly name a virgin, a Messiah, crucifixion, or
Jesus. Its collective language must not be suppressed. [P01]

**2. Blessing reaches the nations through Abraham's offspring.** Genesis 12:1-3; 18:18;
22:18; Galatians 3:8, 16, 29; Acts 3:25-26. Explicit NT application. Paul reads the
promise through Christ and the inclusion of non-Jews. In Genesis, the promise concerns
Abraham's descendants and their relation to other peoples. Paul's argument about a
singular offspring is theological interpretation of a noun that can also designate a
collective. [P02]

**3. The chosen line runs through Isaac.** Genesis 17:19; 21:12; Matthew 1:2; Luke 3:34;
Romans 9:7. Promise plus Christian genealogical application. Genesis distinguishes
Isaac's covenantal line from Abraham's other descendants. The Gospels place Jesus within
it. The claim depends on their genealogies; there is no independently preserved
first-century family register establishing the entire chain.

**4. The line continues through Jacob.** Genesis 28:13-14; Numbers 24:17; Matthew 1:2;
Luke 3:34. Promise and later messianic application. Jacob's descendants inherit land
and become a vehicle of blessing. Christian genealogies locate Jesus within Israel.
Membership in this broad ancestral population cannot by itself uniquely identify one
person as Messiah.

**5. A ruler comes from Judah.** Genesis 49:8-12; Matthew 1:2-3; Hebrews 7:14; Revelation
5:5. Explicit NT identity claim; traditional messianic reading. Jacob's blessing depicts
Judah's superiority and enduring rule. The difficult wording often transliterated Shiloh has
several interpretations. Hebrews directly states Jesus's Judahite ancestry, but Genesis's
historical and literary horizon includes Judah's kingship, not an unambiguous naming of
Jesus.

**6. David's dynasty endures.** 2 Samuel 7:12-16; Psalm 89:3-4, 19-37; Psalm 132:11-12;
Luke 1:32-33; Romans 1:3; Acts 2:30. Explicit NT application. The immediate promise
concerns David's successor and dynasty, including temple building. Christians interpret
Jesus as its definitive heir. The original covenant also concerns human kings who can
commit wrong and require discipline; applying every sentence directly to a sinless Jesus
creates difficulties.

**7. A shoot grows from Jesse's line.** Isaiah 11:1-10; Romans 15:12; Revelation 22:16.
Explicit NT application. Isaiah imagines righteous Davidic rule, judgment for the poor,
and extraordinary peace. Paul uses the passage to support Gentile hope in Christ.

Christians usually distinguish the inauguration of this rule from its final completion; the
passage's worldwide peace has not simply become an observable historical condition. [P03]

**8. The righteous Branch restores just rule.** Jeremiah 23:5-6; 33:14-18; Luke 1:32-33; 1
Corinthians 1:30. Later Christian reading with NT thematic parallels. Jeremiah's
promise answers failed Judean kingship and envisions security for Judah and Israel. The
application to Jesus is traditional, but these New Testament texts do not explicitly quote
Jeremiah as a fulfilled biographical prediction. The passage also retains concrete national
and priestly expectations.

**9. The king is God's son.** Psalm 2:7-9; 2 Samuel 7:14; Acts 13:33; Hebrews 1:5; 5:5.
Explicit NT application. In royal language, divine sonship expresses the king's special
appointment and relation to God. Hebrews and Acts apply these texts to Jesus. Christian
claims about his eternal divine nature go beyond the minimum meaning of a royal title in
its original setting. [P04]

**10. The anointed king has an enduring throne.** Psalm 45:6-7; Hebrews 1:8-9. Explicit
NT application. A royal wedding psalm is read as speech about the Son. Its address to the
king and the translation of the term commonly rendered God are discussed in scholarship.
Hebrews deliberately gives the text a Christological setting; the original poem was not
written as a straightforward life of a future Galilean. [P04]

## 2. Conception, birth, and childhood

**11. The virgin or young woman and Immanuel.** Isaiah 7:14-16; Matthew 1:22-23.
Explicit NT application. Isaiah's sign addresses King Ahaz during an eighth-century BCE
political crisis and provides a near-term horizon within a child's development. Matthew
uses the Greek wording in a narrative of virginal conception. The Hebrew and Greek issues
receive fuller treatment below. This is the leading example of an original historical setting
and a later Christian fulfillment reading. [P05, P06]

**12. Bethlehem as the ruler's origin.** Micah 5:2-5; Matthew 2:5-6; John 7:41-42. Explicit
NT application. Micah expects a ruler associated with David's ancestral Bethlehem who
will secure Israel. Matthew combines Micah's wording with shepherd language comparable
to 2 Samuel 5:2. Ancient origins can denote Davidic antiquity; they do not, by themselves,
settle the doctrine of eternal preexistence. The Gospels' infancy narratives supply the
claimed fulfillment. [P07]

**13. A royal child brings justice and peace.** Isaiah 9:6-7; Luke 1:32-33; 2:11-14.
Traditional messianic reading and NT echoes. Christians associate the child's royal
titles and lasting Davidic government with Jesus. The saying stands within ancient Judean
royal hopes; the precise original royal referent is disputed. The New Testament never
introduces Isaiah 9:6 with a fulfillment formula about Jesus's birth. The promised peace
remains a major interpretive question.

**14. A star rises from Jacob.** Numbers 24:17; Matthew 2:1-12; Revelation 22:16. NT
allusion and later interpretation. Balaam's oracle portrays an Israelite ruler overcoming
enemies. Matthew's guiding star may evoke this royal symbolism, although he does not
quote Numbers. Revelation calls Jesus the morning star. These links do not identify a
specific historical astronomical event, nor establish that a particular conjunction caused
Matthew's story.

**15. Foreigners bring tribute.** Psalm 72:10-11; Isaiah 60:1-6; Matthew 2:1-12. NT
allusion and later Christian reading. Royal homage and nations bringing wealth to Zion
provide a scriptural vocabulary for the magi's gifts. Matthew calls the visitors magi, not
kings, and does not say there were three. The later three kings tradition combines the
three gift types with passages such as Psalm 72.

**16. God's son comes out of Egypt.** Hosea 11:1; Matthew 2:14-15. Explicit NT typology.
Hosea remembers the exodus of Israel, God's son, and then criticizes Israel's subsequent
infidelity. Matthew presents Jesus as recapitulating that history. Calling Hosea's sentence a
direct prediction of an infant's future Egyptian journey ignores its original past-tense
context. Matthew nevertheless explicitly considers the correspondence fulfillment. [P42]

**17. Rachel weeps for her children.** Jeremiah 31:15-17; Matthew 2:16-18. Explicit NT
application. Jeremiah personifies the ancestral mother mourning displaced descendants;
the following verses promise their return. Matthew connects the lament to Herod's killing
of children. Jeremiah does not specify Bethlehem infants, Herod, or a Messiah. The alleged
massacre also requires historical assessment separately from the literary connection.

**18. He will be called a Nazorean.** Matthew 2:23; proposed connections include Isaiah
11:1 and Judges 13:5. Explicit NT fulfillment claim with an unidentified source. No
exact surviving Hebrew Bible passage says this. A wordplay on netser, branch, is plausible
but unproved. Nazorean, associated with Nazareth, should not simply be equated with
Nazirite, a person under a special vow. Matthew refers to prophets in the plural. [P08]

**19. The endangered child repeats Moses's story.** Exodus 1:15-2:10; 4:19; Matthew
2:13-21. Narrative typology. A murderous ruler threatens children; the chosen child
survives; a return follows the death of those seeking his life. Matthew's wording echoes
Exodus. This is a literary pattern, not an additional oracle saying that Jesus would
experience these incidents. Recognition of the pattern neither proves nor automatically
disproves a historical core.

## 3. The forerunner, baptism, and public ministry

**20. Elijah returns before the decisive day.** Malachi 4:5-6, or 3:23-24 in some editions;
Luke 1:17; Matthew 11:14; 17:10-13; John 1:21. Explicit NT application to John the
Baptist. Matthew identifies John with the expected Elijah; Luke specifies Elijah's spirit and
power. John denies being Elijah in the Fourth Gospel. A symbolic vocation rather than
literal personal identity is one Christian explanation of this difference. [P09]

**21. A wilderness voice prepares the Lord's road.** Isaiah 40:3-5; Mark 1:3; Matthew 3:3;
Luke 3:4-6; John 1:23. Explicit NT application. Isaiah announces God's coming and
restoration for an exiled people. The Gospels associate the voice with John and the coming
with Jesus. The transfer is theologically significant: language originally about preparing for
Israel's God becomes part of Jesus's arrival.

**22. A messenger goes ahead.** Malachi 3:1; Exodus 23:20; Mark 1:2; Matthew 11:10;
Luke 7:27. Explicit NT application. The Gospel wording combines scriptural messenger
language. Mark follows it with Isaiah 40:3 under an Isaiah heading, illustrating composite
quotation. Malachi originally speaks about judgment and the purification of worship.
Identifying John and Jesus as the relevant messenger and coming Lord is a Christian
interpretive claim. [P09]

**23. The chosen son receives the Spirit.** Psalm 2:7; Isaiah 42:1; Mark 1:9-11 and
parallels. NT allusion. The baptismal voice and descent of the Spirit evoke royal sonship
and the servant who carries out God's purpose. This combination explains the narrative's
theological vocabulary. Neither earlier passage describes a baptism in the Jordan by a
named John. The voice also has similarities to the beloved-son language of Genesis 22.

**24. Faithful Israel succeeds in the wilderness.** Deuteronomy 6:13, 16; 8:2-3; Matthew
4:1-11; Luke 4:1-13. Explicit quotation within typology. Jesus answers temptation using
instructions originally addressed to Israel in its wilderness testing. Forty days echoes the
larger scriptural pattern of forty. The citations function as guidance Jesus obeys, not
forecasts whose probability can be multiplied as independent fulfilled predictions. [P10]

**25. Light reaches Galilee.** Isaiah 9:1-2, or 8:23-9:1; Matthew 4:13-16. Explicit NT
application. Isaiah's hope answers humiliation of northern territories and anticipates
deliverance. Matthew links it to Jesus's Galilean activity. Geographic correspondence is
real within the narrative, but the larger oracle includes political liberation and royal
justice. It is not merely a prediction that a teacher would reside near Capernaum.

**26. An anointed herald announces good news and release.** Isaiah 61:1-2; 58:6; Luke
4:16-21. Explicit NT application spoken by Jesus in Luke. Jesus reads a composite
scriptural passage and says it is fulfilled for his hearers. Isaiah describes restoration for
Zion's afflicted people. Luke's selection omits the continuation about God's vengeance,
making the scope of the selected text an important part of its interpretation. [P10, P11]

**27. The blind see, the lame walk, and the poor receive news.** Isaiah 35:5-6; 61:1;
compare Isaiah 26:19; Matthew 11:2-6; Luke 7:18-23. NT allusive answer to a messianic
question. Jesus's reported works resemble the signs of restoration. The Dead Sea Scroll
4Q521 also associates end-time renewal with healing, good news, and resurrection; the
cluster belongs within Jewish hopes, rather than appearing from nowhere in Christianity.
[P12]

**28. The servant bears illnesses.** Isaiah 53:4; Matthew 8:16-17. Explicit NT
application. Matthew applies the servant's bearing of sufferings to Jesus's healing

ministry. This is worth distinguishing from later use of the same verse exclusively about
crucifixion. The verse's actual New Testament application here concerns relief of illness.
Identifying the servant with Jesus is the interpretive premise, not a conclusion established
by the healing report alone.

**29. The servant acts gently and brings justice.** Isaiah 42:1-4; Matthew 12:15-21.
Explicit NT application. Matthew uses the servant passage to characterize Jesus's
restraint and mission. In Isaiah, the servant belongs to the wider imagery of Israel and its
representative vocation. The promise includes justice reaching the nations; it is broader
than a prediction that a particular individual will speak quietly. [P13]

**30. A light for the Gentiles.** Isaiah 42:6; 49:6; Luke 2:29-32; Acts 13:47. Explicit NT
application and echo. Simeon's words portray Jesus as light for non-Jews; Acts applies
the servant's commission to Paul and Barnabas as well. Isaiah's restoration of Israel
expands toward other nations. The New Testament itself permits a scriptural vocation to be
realized through both Jesus and the community's mission. [P14]

**31. A prophet like Moses.** Deuteronomy 18:15-19; John 6:14; Acts 3:22-23; 7:37. Explicit
NT application. Deuteronomy provides for prophetic guidance after Moses and forbids
rival divination. Acts identifies Jesus with the expected prophet. The original formulation
can describe the institution or succession of prophets; an exclusive forecast of Jesus is one
later reading, not the only meaning available in Deuteronomy's setting.

**32. Teaching through parables.** Psalm 78:2; Matthew 13:34-35. Explicit NT
application. A psalm announcing instruction through Israel's remembered history
becomes a rationale for Jesus's parabolic teaching. The psalm does not originally say that
an otherwise unknown future Messiah must teach in stories. Matthew reads an existing
mode of sacred instruction as finding a new expression in Jesus. [P15]

**33. Hearers fail to understand.** Isaiah 6:9-10; Mark 4:10-12; Matthew 13:14-15; John
12:39-41; Acts 28:25-27. Explicit NT application. Isaiah's difficult commission becomes a
scriptural explanation of resistant audiences. The New Testament uses the passage in
several settings, so it is not one uniquely identifying incident. Its use also raises theological
questions about divine hardening and human responsibility. [P15]

**34. Lips honor God while hearts remain distant.** Isaiah 29:13; Mark 7:6-7; Matthew
15:7-9. Explicit NT application. Jesus applies an earlier criticism of hollow worship to
opponents in the Gospel narrative. This is prophetic diagnosis reused for a new
controversy. It should not be counted as a precise forecast of particular Pharisees, or
generalized into a judgment about all Jews.

**35. The servant's message encounters disbelief.** Isaiah 53:1; John 12:37-38; Romans
10:16. Explicit NT application. John interprets resistance to Jesus through the servant
poem; Paul uses the same line for the reception of the gospel. Since the saying is broad and
can fit many rejected messengers, its principal force is theological interpretation rather
than unique statistical identification.

**36. Hatred without cause.** Psalm 35:19; 69:4; John 15:24-25. Explicit NT application.
An innocent sufferer's lament becomes Jesus's explanation of hostility. The immediate
psalmic speaker describes his own enemies. The text offers a recognizable pattern of
unjust persecution, but does not give independently testable information about the identity,
dates, or acts of Jesus's opponents.

**37. Estrangement from his family.** Psalm 69:8; Mark 3:20-21, 31-35; John 7:5. Later
Christian reading. The Gospel accounts of family misunderstanding resemble the
psalmist's alienation. No Gospel explicitly calls this verse fulfilled. Psalm 69 also contains a
confession concerning the speaker's wrongdoing in verse 5, showing why a direct
one-to-one application of the entire psalm requires interpretive selection.

**38. The true shepherd gathers the flock.** Ezekiel 34:11-24; Psalm 23; John 10:1-18;
Matthew 9:36. NT allusion. Ezekiel contrasts failed rulers with God's own shepherding
and a Davidic shepherd. John's Jesus takes the role of the good shepherd. The imagery
contributes to both royal and divine Christology, while the original prophecy also concerns
Israel's restoration and security in its land.

**39. Bread from heaven.** Exodus 16; Psalm 78:24; John 6:30-58. Explicit NT typology.
Manna becomes the scriptural starting point for Jesus's identification as life-giving bread.
Exodus narrates provision to Israel; it does not predict a later sermon or the feeding of five
thousand. John recasts the earlier gift in relation to Jesus's person, flesh, death, and the life
of believers.

**40. Water from the wilderness rock.** Exodus 17:1-7; Numbers 20:2-13; 1 Corinthians
10:1-4. Explicit NT typology. Paul identifies the spiritual rock accompanying Israel with
Christ. This is a theological reading of Israel's wilderness experience, not an independent
historical record placing Jesus of Nazareth in the Bronze Age. The surrounding passage
uses Israel's failures to admonish Paul's own community.

**41. The lifted bronze serpent.** Numbers 21:4-9; John 3:14-15. Explicit NT typology.
John compares Moses lifting the serpent with the Son of Man being lifted, anticipating
crucifixion and exaltation. The earlier object heals Israelites who look at it. It is not a
verbal prediction of crucifixion, and the comparison does not mean every feature of the
serpent story applies to Jesus.

**42. Jacob's ladder and the meeting of heaven and earth.** Genesis 28:10-17; John 1:51.
Explicit NT allusion. The angels' movement from Jacob's dream is recast around the Son
of Man. The Gospel presents Jesus as the place of divine-human encounter. Genesis's
promise to Jacob is not independently phrased as a future biography of Jesus.

**43. Jesus as the temple.** 2 Samuel 7:13; Zechariah 6:12-13; John 2:19-22; compare
Revelation 21:22. Typology and later prophetic linkage. John explicitly interprets
Jesus's temple saying as referring to his body and resurrection. Christian readers connect
this to temple-building promises. The earlier passages concern a sanctuary and royal or
priestly restoration; Jesus did not construct a new stone Temple in Jerusalem.

**44. Living water flows outward.** Isaiah 44:3; Ezekiel 47:1-12; Zechariah 14:8; John
7:37-39. Scriptural allusion with an unidentified exact quotation. John invokes
Scripture but supplies no precise verse matching the entire wording. Several
water-and-Spirit passages form the likely background. The narrative interprets the water
as the Spirit received after Jesus's glorification. A single verbatim Old Testament
prediction should not be manufactured. [P16]

**45. Elijah and Elisha as miracle patterns.** 1 Kings 17:8-24; 2 Kings 4:18-44; Luke
4:25-27; 7:11-17; John 6:1-14. Explicit comparison and narrative typology. Prophetic
healings, revived children, and multiplied food provide antecedents for Gospel episodes.
Luke explicitly recalls Elijah and Elisha. Similarity establishes participation in a shared
scriptural world; it does not settle whether a particular later event occurred.

**46. An unimpressive appearance.** Isaiah 53:2-3; compare Philippians 2:6-8. Later
Christian reading. Popular catalogues say Jesus had no physical beauty, but the canonical
Gospels supply no physical portrait confirming that assertion. The servant poem describes
one who fails to command esteem. It should not be presented as verified information about
Jesus's face, height, complexion, or attractiveness.

## 4. Jerusalem, betrayal, trial, and crucifixion

**47. The king enters on a donkey.** Zechariah 9:9-10; Matthew 21:4-5; John 12:14-16;
compare Mark 11:1-10. Explicit NT application. Zechariah depicts a humble or afflicted
king and worldwide peace. Jesus's narrated entry acts out the royal sign. Matthew
mentions a donkey and colt, while Mark centers on one colt; Hebrew poetic parallelism
helps explain the issue. A deliberate symbolic action cannot be treated as a random
coincidence. [P17]

**48. Acclamation of the one who comes.** Psalm 118:25-26; Mark 11:9-10 and parallels.
Explicit scriptural acclamation. Festival language of welcome and salvation is applied to
Jesus entering Jerusalem. The psalm originally celebrates deliverance and worship. The
crowds' use of existing sacred words is evidence of the narrative's interpretation of Jesus;
it is not an independently surprising prediction of the words they would choose.

**49. Praise from children.** Psalm 8:2; Matthew 21:15-16. Explicit NT application. Jesus
answers criticism of children's praise with a psalmic line. Matthew follows the Greek
wording about praise, where the Hebrew wording is commonly translated strength. A
statement about God's power displayed through the weak becomes an explanation of
Jesus's reception. The translation history affects the exact verbal correspondence.

**50. The temple should be a house of prayer.** Isaiah 56:6-8; Mark 11:17; Matthew
21:13; Luke 19:46. Explicit quotation. Isaiah welcomes faithful outsiders into worship;
Mark preserves the phrase concerning all nations. Jesus invokes the text during the temple
action. This is an appeal to a scriptural standard, not a separate prediction that someone
would overturn tables.

**51. A den of robbers.** Jeremiah 7:1-15; Mark 11:17 and parallels. Explicit quotation and
prophetic reenactment. Jeremiah condemns people who commit wrong and then treat
the sanctuary as security. Jesus's quotation connects his criticism with that earlier
warning. The image concerns refuge for wrongdoers, so reducing it entirely to unfair
prices in a religious marketplace misses its wider meaning.

**52. Zeal for God's house.** Psalm 69:9; John 2:13-17; compare Romans 15:3. Explicit NT
application. John says the disciples remember the psalm when Jesus acts in the temple.
Paul applies another clause of the verse to Christ's bearing reproach. These are distinct
uses of one lament, not two independently predicted biographical facts. John's temple
episode is positioned earlier than the Synoptic one.

**53. The Lord comes to his temple.** Malachi 3:1-4; compare Luke 2:22-38; Mark
11:11-17. Later Christian application of a prophetic oracle. Christians associate the
arrival and purification imagery with Jesus. Malachi envisages purified Levites and
acceptable offerings, which do not correspond straightforwardly to abolishing temple
worship. The New Testament explicitly applies Malachi's preceding messenger language,
but does not make every later temple identification explicit. [P09]

**54. The rejected stone becomes foundational.** Psalm 118:22-23; Mark 12:10-11; Acts
4:11; 1 Peter 2:7. Explicit NT application. An image of reversal after rejection becomes a
statement about Jesus's rejection and vindication. The psalm's original stone metaphor
does not identify a crucifixion, date, or person named Jesus. The connection is nonetheless
central and unambiguous within early Christian interpretation.

**55. The stone causes stumbling and offers security.** Isaiah 8:14-15; 28:16; Romans
9:32-33; 1 Peter 2:6-8. Explicit NT application. Early Christian writers combine passages
about a sanctuary or stumbling stone and a secure foundation in Zion. They use them to
describe divided responses to Christ. The original texts address trust and political-religious
choices in Judah, not a plainly stated future Gospel episode.

**56. Betrayal by a table companion.** Psalm 41:9; John 13:18. Explicit NT application.
John applies the afflicted psalmist's broken friendship to Judas. The psalm describes a
suffering speaker who also asks forgiveness for sin; Christian interpretation selects the
betrayal motif rather than making every statement Jesus's autobiography. Its specificity is
literary and relational, not a naming of Judas centuries in advance.

**57. Betrayal by an intimate friend.** Psalm 55:12-14, 20-21; compare Matthew 26:47-50.
Later Christian reading. The psalm's anguish over a companion's treachery is frequently
associated with Judas's kiss. The Gospel does not explicitly cite it. Counting this alongside
Psalm 41 as a second statistically independent forecast mistakes two similar laments for
unrelated measurements of one event.

**58. Thirty pieces of silver.** Zechariah 11:12-13; Matthew 26:14-16; 27:3-10. Explicit NT
application. Zechariah's symbolic shepherd receives a contemptuous wage. Matthew
connects that amount with Judas's payment. Zechariah does not explicitly describe a

disciple betraying a Messiah to Roman execution. The thirty-piece detail is reported in
Matthew and requires assessment within Matthew's already scriptural narration. [P18]

**59. Money in the temple and the potter's field.** Zechariah 11:13; Jeremiah 18:1-3;
19:1-13; 32:6-9; Matthew 27:5-10. Explicit fulfillment claim with composite or
problematic attribution. Matthew names Jeremiah, although the clearest verbal source
is Zechariah. Combining Jeremiah's potter, field, and judgment motifs is a common
explanation; a simple mistaken attribution remains a historical possibility. No extant
Jeremiah verse contains Matthew's whole quotation. [P18]

**60. The shepherd is struck and the sheep scatter.** Zechariah 13:7; Mark 14:27;
Matthew 26:31. Explicit NT application. Jesus interprets his arrest and his followers'
dispersal through Zechariah. The earlier oracle concerns a shepherd's death and the
purification of a people. Its historical referent is debated. This is a strong example of the
Gospels' own interpretive claim, rather than a connection invented only by later apologists.

**61. False witnesses testify.** Psalm 35:11; compare Mark 14:55-59; Matthew 26:59-61.
Later Christian reading or general allusion. An innocent sufferer's complaint
resembles the trial narrative. Neither Gospel marks this particular verse as fulfilled. False
accusation is also a recurrent ancient experience; a resemblance of this kind supplies weak
independent identifying information.

**62. Striking, humiliation, and spitting.** Isaiah 50:6; Mark 14:65; 15:19; Matthew 26:67;
27:30. NT allusion. The obedient servant's humiliation provides a close scriptural pattern.
The Gospels report blows and spitting, but do not report Jesus's beard being plucked,
although that detail occurs in Isaiah and is often copied into prophecy lists as if separately
attested.

**63. Silence before accusers.** Isaiah 53:7; Mark 14:61; 15:5; Matthew 27:12-14; Acts
8:32-35. NT allusion and explicit Christian application. Acts directly identifies the
slaughtered, silent servant with Jesus. The Gospels also record Jesus speaking during
interrogations, so the correspondence concerns restraint and refusal to answer particular
charges, not absolute silence at every stage.

**64. Rulers gather against the anointed one.** Psalm 2:1-2; Acts 4:25-28. Explicit NT
application. Acts identifies Herod, Pilate, Gentiles, and some people of Israel as
participants in the opposition. Psalm 2 originally concerns rebellion against God's installed
king. The passage is evidence for the early movement's interpretation of the passion; it is
not a license to assign inherited guilt to any later population.

**65. The righteous son is tested by a shameful death.** Wisdom of Solomon 2:12-20;
Matthew 27:43. Probable NT allusion and Christian typology. Wisdom depicts the
wicked testing whether God will rescue a righteous man who claims divine sonship.
Matthew's mockery closely resembles the pattern. Wisdom is in Catholic and Orthodox Old
Testaments but not the Jewish Tanakh or most Protestant Old Testaments. It is not an
explicit named prediction of Jesus. [P19]

**66. Mockery and challenges to divine rescue.** Psalm 22:7-8; Mark 15:29-32; Matthew
27:39-43. NT allusion. The passion narratives use the language of the suffering psalmist.
Matthew's rescue taunt is particularly close. This demonstrates the influence of Psalm 22
on the narrative's presentation. Whether a historical taunt was remembered, shaped by the
psalm, or both cannot be decided from resemblance alone. [P43]

**67. The cry of abandonment.** Psalm 22:1; Mark 15:34; Matthew 27:46. Explicit
quotation attributed to Jesus. A psalmic plea becomes Jesus's cry on the cross.
Christians differ over whether the opening invokes the whole psalm's eventual vindication
or emphasizes actual desolation. The text supports a connection with Jewish lament; it does
not identify Jesus as someone outside Judaism. [P44]

**68. Hands and feet pierced.** Psalm 22:16, Hebrew verse 17; compare Luke 24:39-40;
John 20:25-27. Later explicit Christian interpretation; not a direct NT quotation of
this verse. Christian translations often say pierced; the received Hebrew reads like a lion,
with difficult syntax, while Greek has a digging or piercing expression. The textual issue is
discussed below. Nail wounds are reported, but the psalm's exact wording is contested.
[P21]

**69. Clothes divided and lots cast.** Psalm 22:18; Mark 15:24; John 19:23-24. Explicit NT
application in John. The psalm's parallel poetic lines become two related actions
involving clothing and a seamless tunic. The correspondence is detailed, but John tells the
reader that Scripture structures its meaning. Four Gospel versions are not automatically
four independent attestations of this detail. [P22]

**70. Extreme thirst.** Psalm 22:15; John 19:28. Possible NT allusion. John says Jesus
expresses thirst so Scripture will be completed, without naming the passage. Psalm 69:21
is another likely source because the account immediately supplies sour wine. Thirst is also
an ordinary consequence of severe suffering; the text does not create a uniquely identifying
physiological test.

**71. Gall or sour wine.** Psalm 69:21; Matthew 27:34, 48; Mark 15:23, 36; John 19:28-30.
NT allusion and fulfillment context. Gospel details differ: Matthew mentions gall, Mark
an earlier drink mixed with myrrh, and sour wine appears later. The lament speaks of
hostile treatment of a sufferer. The narrative differences should be preserved instead of
reporting one artificially harmonized recipe.

**72. Entrusting his spirit to God.** Psalm 31:5; Luke 23:46. Explicit quotation. Luke
gives Jesus a prayer of trust from the Psalms at his death. The psalm originally voices
dependence on God amid danger. Jesus's use of a familiar prayer would be meaningful
without requiring the original prayer to have been composed as a prediction of his exact
final words.

**73. No bone is broken.** Exodus 12:46; Numbers 9:12; Psalm 34:20; John 19:33-36.
Explicit NT fulfillment claim. John presents unbroken bones as scriptural fulfillment,
with both Passover-lamb instructions and protection of the righteous as relevant

backgrounds. The law concerns treatment of an actual sacrificial animal. John makes the
link to Jesus; it is not an independently identified remains examination. [P22]

**74. The pierced figure is seen and mourned.** Zechariah 12:10; John 19:34-37;
Revelation 1:7. Explicit NT application. John connects a piercing with the crucifixion;
Revelation applies it to future manifestation. Zechariah describes mourning around an
unnamed pierced figure in a national restoration setting. Pronouns and ancient textual
forms complicate claims that the wording unambiguously predicts God's own crucifixion.
[P23]

**75. Darkness at noon.** Amos 8:9-10; Mark 15:33; Matthew 27:45; Luke 23:44-45.
Possible NT allusion. Amos uses darkened daylight and mourning for an only son as
judgment imagery. The Gospels report darkness but do not explicitly identify Amos as its
source. A reported event, a literary judgment sign, and a proposed astronomical
explanation are separate questions.

**76. Counted among transgressors.** Isaiah 53:12; Luke 22:37; 23:32-33. Explicit NT
application. Luke's Jesus directly identifies himself with the servant passage before
arrest. The later execution beside others fits the theme. Mark 15:28, another explicit
fulfillment statement in some Bibles, is absent from important early manuscripts and is
generally treated as a secondary addition, so it is not secure independent early evidence.
[P43]

**77. Burial associated with a rich man.** Isaiah 53:9; Matthew 27:57-60. NT allusion
and later explicit Christian interpretation. Matthew calls Joseph of Arimathea rich and
places Jesus in Joseph's tomb. The Hebrew of Isaiah's burial line is difficult, and
translations vary. The connection should not turn all Gospel descriptions of Joseph into
independent claims about his wealth; that detail is Matthew's. [P24]

## 5. The theological meaning of the death

**78. The servant suffers for others.** Isaiah 52:13-53:12; Mark 10:45; Acts 8:30-35; 1
Peter 2:22-25; Hebrews 9:28. Explicit NT application and allusion. The servant's
suffering, innocence, and benefit to others become foundational Christian explanations of
Jesus's death. Isaiah's servant identity and the meaning of the suffering remain interpretive
questions. The New Testament's use is early and substantial; the claim that everyone
before Christianity already agreed on this reading is false. [P25]

**79. The Passover lamb.** Exodus 12; 1 Corinthians 5:7; John 19:14, 36; compare John 1:29.
Explicit NT typology. Paul directly identifies Christ with Passover. The ritual remembers
liberation from Egypt; Christianity rereads Jesus's death as a new liberation. John's
chronology and lamb imagery reinforce the pattern. A type can be powerful without being a
predictive sentence specifying the future date and mechanics of a crucifixion.

**80. Isaac, the beloved son offered and restored.** Genesis 22:1-19; Romans 8:32;
Hebrews 11:17-19. Typology, with NT verbal and conceptual parallels. The binding of
Isaac supplies a father-son sacrifice pattern; Hebrews already calls Isaac's reception back
figurative. Genesis actually stops the sacrifice. It neither explicitly names Jesus nor says
God must sacrifice a son in exactly the same circumstances.

**81. The Day of Atonement and sacrificial access.** Leviticus 16; Hebrews 9:6-14, 24-28;
10:1-18; later Epistle of Barnabas 7. Explicit NT and early Christian typology. Hebrews
interprets Christ's heavenly entry and self-offering through priestly ritual. Barnabas makes
additional scapegoat associations. Different animals and rituals should not be merged into
one literal description: Christian authors adapt a complex sacrificial system to explain one
death.

**82. The curse of hanging on a tree.** Deuteronomy 21:22-23; Galatians 3:13; Acts 5:30.
Explicit NT application. Deuteronomy regulates exposure of an executed person's body.
Paul reinterprets Jesus's execution as bearing a curse on behalf of others. The law is a
general instruction, not a prediction that the Messiah would be crucified; its application is
part of Paul's explanation of an already proclaimed death. [P02]

**83. An anointed one is cut off.** Daniel 9:24-27; compare Matthew 24:15; Mark 13:14.
Later Christian chronological identification; partial explicit NT reuse of the
chapter. Daniel describes periods of restoration, an anointed one's removal, and
desecration. The Gospels explicitly reuse the desolating-abomination motif, but never
calculate a 483-year countdown to Jesus. The dominant critical contextual reading
concerns the second-century BCE Antiochene crisis. [P26]

**84. A new covenant.** Jeremiah 31:31-34; Luke 22:20; 1 Corinthians 11:25; Hebrews
8:8-12; 10:16-17. Explicit NT application. Jeremiah promises renewed covenant with
Israel and Judah, forgiveness, and Torah internalized. Christian writers identify Jesus as its
mediator. Jeremiah does not announce the title of a future Christian collection of books,
and the persistence of Israel and Judah in the oracle matters for interpretation. [P27]

**85. Covenant ratified through blood.** Exodus 24:3-8; Mark 14:24; Matthew 26:28;
Hebrews 9:18-22. Explicit NT typology and verbal echo. The Sinai covenant ceremony
supplies language for Jesus's cup and death. The original passage reports a ceremony
involving Israel, not a forecast of a last supper. Its reuse connects Jesus's death with the
establishment of a community bound to God.

**86. Obedience and a prepared body.** Psalm 40:6-8; Hebrews 10:5-10. Explicit NT
application. Hebrews uses the Greek form, which refers to a body prepared, whereas the
received Hebrew refers to ears opened or dug. The earlier psalm values doing God's will;
Hebrews makes it speech of Christ entering the world. This is an important example of a
Christological argument dependent on a particular ancient textual form. [P28]

## 6. Resurrection, exaltation, and the continuing mission

**87. Revival on the third day.** Hosea 6:1-3; compare 1 Corinthians 15:4; Luke 24:46.
Possible scriptural background, not an explicit NT quotation of Hosea. Hosea's
speakers expect restoration for Israel. Paul says resurrection occurred according to the
Scriptures but does not specify which verse. Identifying Hosea as the unique intended text
exceeds what Paul actually tells us. Other scriptural third-day patterns also influenced
Christian interpretation. [P29]

**88. Jonah's three days.** Jonah 1:17; Matthew 12:39-40; compare Luke 11:29-32. Explicit
NT typology. Matthew compares Jonah's time inside the sea creature with the Son of Man
in the earth. Luke emphasizes Jonah's sign to Nineveh without making the same duration
comparison. A Friday-to-Sunday chronology requires a discussion of inclusive reckoning; it
is not seventy-two elapsed hours. The stories should not be collapsed without explanation.

**89. The holy one does not undergo corruption.** Psalm 16:8-11; Acts 2:25-32; 13:35-37.
Explicit NT application. Acts argues that David died whereas Jesus was raised, making
the psalm refer beyond David. In its original literary setting the speaker expresses
confidence that God will preserve his life; whether it originally envisioned resurrection
after death is debated. The Greek wording shapes the argument about decay. [P31]

**90. The suffering servant sees life and vindication.** Isaiah 53:10-12; compare the
Christological reading of the servant in Acts 8:35 and 1 Peter 2:21-25. Christian reading
of the servant's vindication. Seeing offspring, prolonging days, and seeing light are
interpreted through Jesus's risen life and his community. Isaiah does not explicitly state a
resurrection on a third day. The terms can also support collective or representative
interpretations of the servant. [P24]

**91. David's enduring promises become effective.** Isaiah 55:3; Acts 13:34. Explicit NT
application. Acts uses the reliable promises associated with David as part of an argument
about resurrection. Isaiah offers covenantal renewal to the restored people. The linkage
depends on the broader Christian identification of Jesus as David's heir; it is not an isolated
oracle specifying an empty tomb.

**92. Death is finally defeated.** Isaiah 25:8; Hosea 13:14; 1 Corinthians 15:54-57. Explicit
NT application to future resurrection. Paul associates Jesus's victory with the eventual
defeat of death for believers. This is not a claim that bodily death ceased throughout the
world in the first century. Paul's argument has a future collective dimension, which a list of
already completed prophecies must preserve. [P30]

**93. The vindicated one joins his brothers in praise.** Psalm 22:22; Hebrews 2:11-12.
Explicit NT application. Hebrews gives the psalm's thankful speech to Christ and
connects it with his solidarity with believers. The psalm itself moves from distress toward
public praise. Christianity reads that whole movement through Jesus's suffering and
vindication, rather than treating only the painful opening as relevant.

**94. Enthronement at God's right hand.** Psalm 110:1; Mark 12:35-37; 14:62; Acts
2:34-36; Hebrews 1:13. Explicit NT application. A royal enthronement text becomes one
of Christianity's most important accounts of Jesus's exaltation. The psalm originally speaks
to or about a ruler beside God's authority. The New Testament's theological conclusion is
clear; historians cannot independently observe a heavenly enthronement. [P32]

**95. The humanlike figure receives dominion.** Daniel 7:13-14, 18, 27; Mark 14:62;
Matthew 24:30; Revelation 1:7, 13. Explicit NT application and allusion. Daniel's vision
contrasts beastly empires with a humanlike recipient of divine rule and interprets kingship
in relation to the holy ones. Christians identify Jesus as the representative Son of Man.
Whether the figure was originally individual, angelic, corporate, or representative remains
debated. [P33]

**96. A permanent priest in Melchizedek's order.** Genesis 14:18-20; Psalm 110:4;
Hebrews 5:5-10; 7:1-28. Explicit NT typology and application. Hebrews uses
Melchizedek's scriptural portrayal to explain a priesthood independent of Levitical
ancestry. Silence about his genealogy in Genesis becomes interpretively significant.
Genesis does not plainly identify Melchizedek as Jesus; that identification goes beyond
Hebrews's comparison. [P34]

**97. Ascension and gifts.** Psalm 68:18; Ephesians 4:7-13. Explicit NT application. A
divine victory and ascent image is applied to Christ. Ephesians speaks of giving gifts where
the earlier text speaks of receiving gifts, illustrating interpretive adaptation. The
surrounding discussion concerns the equipping of the church; the psalm did not originally
describe the geographic departure from the Mount of Olives. [P35]

**98. The renewed Adam and restored human vocation.** Genesis 1:26-28; 2:7; Psalm
8:4-6; Romans 5:12-21; 1 Corinthians 15:21-28, 45-49; Hebrews 2:5-9. Explicit NT
typology. Christ reverses Adam's legacy and fulfills humanity's intended dominion.
Genesis and Psalm 8 originally concern human beings and their relation to creation. Paul
and Hebrews turn that anthropology into an account of Christ and humanity's future. [P30]

**99. The Spirit is poured out.** Joel 2:28-32, or 3:1-5; Acts 2:16-21. Explicit NT
application to Pentecost. Acts interprets the community's experience through Joel's
promised divine outpouring. Joel also includes cosmic signs and a day of judgment.
Applying its first elements to Pentecost does not establish that every associated
eschatological image had already been literally completed.

**100. David's fallen tent is restored and Gentiles are included.** Amos 9:11-12; Acts
15:13-18. Explicit NT application to the post-Jesus movement. Acts uses the passage
to support Gentile inclusion. The Greek textual tradition concerns other peoples seeking
God; the received Hebrew concerns possessing the remnant of Edom. The difference helps
explain how an oracle of restoration became an argument about a multinational messianic
community. [P36]

## 7. Four disputes that determine how the catalogue is read

Isaiah 7:14: whose child, and what does the word mean?
Read Isaiah 7:1-17 before isolating verse 14. Ahaz fears hostile kings; the sign addresses
that danger, and the child's development supplies a short historical horizon. Hebrew almah
means a young woman of marriageable age; the word alone does not specify the
miraculous absence of sexual intercourse. The Septuagint renders it parthenos, commonly
virgin or maiden. Matthew's own narrative plainly intends a virginal conception and uses
that Greek wording. [P05, P06]

Several Christian approaches are possible: a direct long-range prediction, an initial
historical realization followed by a fuller one, or a typological reading in which divine
presence reaches its climax in Jesus. These are different claims. The textual facts establish
that Matthew reads Isaiah Christologically; they do not erase Isaiah's address to Ahaz.
Calling the passage either an obvious modern prediction or a meaningless connection skips
the actual interpretive problem.

Isaiah 53: individual, Israel, remnant, prophet, or Messiah?
The servant poem begins at Isaiah 52:13. Elsewhere in this part of Isaiah, Israel is
explicitly called God's servant, including 41:8 and 49:3. Yet the servant sometimes acts
toward Israel, making the relation between people, representative, and individual complex.
An individual servant, a faithful subgroup, a prophet, or personified Israel are not
interchangeable theories. Christian reading identifies Jesus's unjust suffering and
subsequent exaltation with the poem's pattern; Acts 8 is an especially explicit witness to
that identification. [P14, P24]

Jewish interpretation has also been diverse. Sanhedrin 98b applies Isaiah 53:4 to a
suffering messianic figure. That does not mean the passage endorses Jesus. Conversely, the
claim that Rashi invented a collective Israel reading in the Middle Ages is refuted by
Origen's Contra Celsum 1.55: already in the third century, Origen reports Jewish
interpreters who understood the servant as the people in dispersion. [P37, P38]

No fair account should omit the poem's compelling resemblance to Christian passion
theology. No fair account should present that resemblance as though the poem named
Jesus, supplied his dates, or permitted only one reading. One also has to ask whether
events generated the scriptural interpretation, the scriptural interpretation shaped the
narration, or both processes operated.

Psalm 22: did the Hebrew say pierced?
The received Hebrew wording in verse 17, usually English verse 16, contains ka'ari, like a
lion, in a syntactically difficult clause about hands and feet. The ancient Greek version has
a verb meaning dug, often rendered pierced in this context. A fragment from Nahal Hever
is important to the debate, but its lettering, spelling, and the proposed underlying Hebrew
form do not remove every difficulty. A scholarly defense of pierced exists; other scholars
defend alternative readings or reconstruct corruption in the transmitted text. [P21]

Two conclusions survive the dispute. First, the psalm is a lament of extreme distress
followed by vindication, not an explicit description of a named Roman execution. Second,
the New Testament quotes and alludes to several of its lines but never directly quotes the
hands-and-feet clause as a fulfillment formula. Justin's later interpretation makes the
crucifixion connection explicit. The textual history is more complicated than either
Christians fabricated the entire wording or modern archaeology proved crucifixion was
spelled out beyond dispute. [P20]

Daniel 9: a calendar predicting Jesus's death?
Daniel reinterprets Jeremiah's seventy years through seventy periods of seven.
Historical-critical readings generally associate the final crisis with Antiochus IV, the killing
of the high priest Onias III in 171 BCE, and the desecration of the Temple in 167-164 BCE.
Anointed can describe a priest or ruler and need not mean the final eschatological Messiah.
[P26]

Christian calculations select different starting decrees, such as the Persian-era rebuilding
permissions associated with Cyrus, Ezra, or Nehemiah; different endpoints, such as
baptism, Jerusalem entry, or crucifixion; and sometimes different year lengths or a gap
before the final week. The arithmetic may be internally consistent once those choices are
made. The choices themselves need defending. Daniel does not say to use one particular
modern conversion, name Jesus, or explicitly identify a crucifixion date.

The chapter is therefore a major ancient apocalyptic text with an extensive Christian
reception, not a universally agreed chronological demonstration. A reader should be shown
the proposed start date, calendar, inclusive-counting rules, punctuation, ending event, and
treatment of the seventieth week before being told that the date is exact.

## 8. Important additional readings that should not become fabricated predictions

• The creator language applied to the Son: Psalm 102:25-27 in Hebrews 1:10-12, and
  angelic worship in Hebrews 1:6 using scriptural Greek traditions related to Deuteronomy
  32:43 and Psalm 97:7. These are major Christological applications. They describe who
  the author believes Jesus is, not separately documented earthly incidents. [P04]

• Wisdom before creation: Proverbs 8:22-31, Wisdom 7:22-8:1, John 1:1-18, and 1
  Corinthians 1:24 inform Christian reflection on preexistence. Personified Wisdom is not a
  literal future birth announcement. Debates over created Wisdom later affected
  arguments about the Son.

• Job's redeemer: Job 19:25-27 became important in Christian resurrection
  interpretation, but its difficult Hebrew and the identity or role of the vindicator are
  disputed. No New Testament passage explicitly declares that Job here predicted Jesus of
  Nazareth.

• Wounds in a friend's house: Zechariah 13:6 is sometimes applied to Jesus's wounds. In
  the immediate context, the speaker belongs to a discussion of discredited prophets. It
  cannot responsibly be quoted while omitting verses 2-5.

• The desired one of the nations: Haggai 2:6-9 has been read as a title of Christ. The
  Hebrew is commonly understood as the valuable possessions of the nations coming to
  adorn the temple. Hebrews 12:26-27 uses the shaking motif, but the New Testament
  does not identify an arriving person in Haggai 2:7 as Jesus.

• Joseph betrayed and exalted: Genesis 37-50 supplies a strong Christian type. Acts 7
  retells Joseph within Israel's history. Numerous proposed parallels do not become
  numerous independent predictions simply because a later chart separates them.

• Intercession for transgressors: Isaiah 53:12 contributes to Christian accounts of
  Jesus's intercession, including Hebrews 7:25. Luke 23:34 supplies the prayer for
  executioners in many manuscripts, but its omission in other early witnesses means that
  its textual status must be acknowledged.

• Judas's vacant office: Acts 1:20 applies Psalm 69:25 and Psalm 109:8 to Judas and his
  replacement. This is another explicit apostolic reuse of older laments, not independent
  evidence that those psalms originally described a disciple named Judas.

• The new community: Hosea 1:10 and 2:23 are reapplied in Romans 9:25-26 and 1 Peter
  2:10; Isaiah 65:1-2 in Romans 10:20-21; Isaiah 54:13 in John 6:45. Originally addressing
  Israel and its relationship to God, these texts explain the community's inclusion and
  instruction. They extend the catalogue beyond discrete incidents in Jesus's life.

• Numbers, names, codes, and calendar combinations: acrostics, Bible-code searches,
  and numerical associations can produce patterns after the target is known. A genuine
  evidential test would need a predeclared rule, independently fixed data, and comparison
  with how often similar patterns appear elsewhere.

## 10. Expectations not straightforwardly fulfilled in Jesus's earthly life

Any serious assessment must include the expected outcomes as well as selected
correspondences. Isaiah 2:2-4 and Micah 4:1-4 describe an end to war; Isaiah 11 envisages
universal knowledge of God and extraordinary peace; Jeremiah 23 associates righteous
kingship with security for Israel and Judah; Ezekiel 37:21-28 joins regathering, one ruler,
and a lasting sanctuary. These are not plainly the political and social conditions achieved
during Jesus's public ministry.

Most Christianity answers through an inaugurated but incomplete kingdom: Jesus's first
coming begins fulfillment, and his return completes it. Jewish objections commonly hold
that the decisive messianic work must actually be accomplished before the claimant is
recognized. Maimonides's Kings and Wars 11 provides an influential medieval formulation
involving a Davidic ruler, restoration of Torah observance, the Temple, and Israel's
ingathering. This is a specific Jewish legal formulation, not a claim that every Jewish
person in every period expected precisely the same sequence. [P40]

Other unresolved horizons include the general resurrection and defeat of death in 1
Corinthians 15, universal acknowledgment in Philippians 2:10-11, Israel's salvation in
Romans 11:25-29, and the renewed creation of Revelation 21-22. Christian schools
distribute these differently across past, present, and future. Modern claims that a war,
technology, political leader, or the founding of Israel in 1948 fulfills a particular saying
require their own arguments. None follows merely from placing a contemporary headline
next to an ancient verse.

## 11. What can the prophecy evidence actually establish?

?
The Dead Sea Scrolls decisively prevent the entire Christian prophecy argument from
being dismissed as Christians inventing Isaiah after Jesus. The Israel Museum dates the
Great Isaiah Scroll to approximately 125 BCE; it contains the book's full sequence of
chapters. Earlier scriptural texts genuinely existed before the Christian movement. That
establishes textual priority. It does not automatically establish which referent a passage
originally had or whether a later event was supernatural. [P41]

A useful assessment asks five separate questions: Was the earlier text fixed before the
event? What did it mean in its original context? Did it specify something precise and
unlikely? Is the event independently well attested? Could actors or narrators who knew the
text intentionally realize or emphasize the correspondence? The answer can differ for every
row above.

This also explains why popular probability demonstrations fail to provide a rigorous proof.
One cannot responsibly multiply invented odds for ancestry, birthplace, betrayal, payment,
and execution as though all were independent random experiments. Several claims derive
from the same Gospel; several details from one earlier poem; some are actions a
participant could intentionally perform; and some are selected after the outcome is known.
A large denominator does not repair ungrounded input probabilities.

For Christian theology, fulfillment can mean that Israel's history acquires a coherent
culmination in Jesus through pattern, promise, suffering, and vindication. That is a serious
claim about the meaning of a scriptural whole. For historical demonstration, the narrower
secure conclusion is that Jesus's followers interpreted him intensely through Jewish
Scripture from the earliest surviving Christian sources onward. Moving from that
conclusion to divine foresight requires additional philosophical and theological judgments
that the textual correspondences alone cannot compel.
