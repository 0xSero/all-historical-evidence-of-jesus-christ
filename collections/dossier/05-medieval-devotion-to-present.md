# From medieval devotion to the present

## What "Jesus's activity after his lifetime" means in the records

The New Testament itself extends Jesus's activity beyond his earthly ministry: appearances
and commissions; the ascension; heavenly intercession; the sending of the Spirit; visions to
Stephen, Paul, and John; and an expected return. These claims are indexed in the life
catalogue (Acts 1-2; 7:55-56; 9; 18:9-10; 22; 23:11; Romans 8:34; Hebrews 7:25;
Revelation). Christian traditions also understand baptism, Eucharist, prayer, healing, and
moral transformation as ways the living Christ acts. Those are theological accounts of
continuing agency rather than a newly recoverable travel diary.

Later claimed encounters belong in the historical record because people reported them
and communities responded. The fact of a reported vision, its impact, and its supernatural
origin remain distinct questions. The examples below are selected for historical
importance, not presented as an exhaustive register of every mystic or apparition.

## Fourteenth to seventeenth centuries: visions, reform, and Christ's presence

1373: Julian of Norwich. Julian's Revelations of Divine Love reflects on a series of visions
associated with a severe illness, including sustained contemplation of the crucified Christ
and divine love. The work became an important source of Christian mystical theology. Its
testimony is evidence for Julian's experience and interpretation; historians cannot
independently observe the encounter she describes. [A33]

Sixteenth century: the Reformations. Major Western Christian disputes centered on
scripture and tradition, justification, the Church, and the Eucharist. Catholics, Lutherans,
Reformed Christians, Anglicans, Anabaptists, and later groups could share much classical
Christology while disagreeing sharply about how Christ saves and how he is present in
worship. The expansion of printed Bibles and scholarly editions changed access to the
sources. This period concerns interpretation and institutions, not the discovery of a new
first-century Gospel. The much broader political history of every denomination falls outside
this dossier's biographical and evidentiary remit.

1673-1675: Margaret Mary Alacoque. Her reported encounters with Jesus became
central to the development of Sacred Heart devotion. The Catholic tradition understands

this devotion through Christ's human and divine love; Pope Francis reviewed this history in
the 2024 encyclical Dilexit nos. The document is an authoritative source for the Church's
interpretation of the devotion, not a laboratory verification of the visions. [A34]

## Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: new histories and new revelations

1757 and subsequent publication: Emanuel Swedenborg. Swedenborg interpreted a
last judgment and a new divine dispensation as events in the spiritual world. His True
Christian Religion 115 associates the new redemption with 1757 and the Lord's second
coming. This is a documented theological claim with a spiritual location; it is not an
independently observed public return of Jesus to first-century-style earthly life. [A41]

Late eighteenth century: Ann Lee and the Shakers. The United Society of Believers in
Christ's Second Appearing understood Ann Lee in relation to Christ's second manifestation
and the feminine dimension of redemption. Their communal life and testimony generated a
distinctive Christian movement. This is relevant to the history of Christ claims, while
remaining a much later interpretation rather than an additional ancient biography. [A56]

1770s onward: the modern historical quest. The posthumous publication of Hermann
Samuel Reimarus's arguments helped make the difference between Jesus's intentions and
the later proclamation of his followers a central scholarly problem. His particular
reconstruction is not accepted merely because it helped launch a discipline. David
Friedrich Strauss's Life of Jesus, Critically Examined (1835-1836) gave myth and
communal interpretation a major role. Ernest Renan's Life of Jesus (1863) offered a widely
read literary-historical portrait. These authors also reflected the philosophical, social, and
sometimes national or racial assumptions of their own eras. [A45][A48]

1830: the Book of Mormon. The first edition appeared in March 1830. Third Nephi
describes the resurrected Jesus appearing to peoples in the Americas, teaching, healing,
instituting rites, and commissioning disciples. Latter-day Saints receive the work as
ancient scripture restored through Joseph Smith. Publicly accessible historical attestation
begins in the nineteenth century; the claimed ancient events should therefore be
distinguished from the book's publication history. Its existence is firm evidence of a major
new scriptural claim, while its ancient origin is a separate question. [A36][A37]

1844: the Millerite disappointment. Many followers expected Christ's return on 22
October 1844. The expected public event did not occur, producing what the movement
itself called the Great Disappointment. Subsequent groups developed different
explanations, including interpretations of heavenly events. Seventh-day Adventism
emerged from part of this history, while continuing to expect a future return. This is a
concrete example of why a prediction's original meaning and its reinterpretation after the
expected date should both remain visible. [A57]

Nineteenth century: the Baha'i interpretation. Baha'i teaching recognizes Jesus as a
Manifestation of God within a sequence of divine messengers. This preserves strong
reverence for Christ while interpreting revelation progressively. It constitutes a living
religious interpretation of Jesus rather than another first-century source. [A39]

1866 onward: Christian Science. Mary Baker Eddy's movement interprets healing and
salvation through its distinction between Jesus and the Christ and its understanding of
divine reality. Its own statements explicitly identify Jesus as central to its practice. The
history belongs to the modern reception of Jesus and religious healing, not to independent
verification of a particular Galilean cure. [A40]

1894 and early twentieth-century "lost years" literature. Nicolas Notovitch's Life of
Issa claim placed Jesus in India and Tibet; later esoteric works developed related travel
narratives or claimed access to hidden spiritual records. The source register discusses the
major examples. Their evidentiary date is when the texts can actually be traced. A detailed
story filling an undocumented period is not strengthened merely by the size of the gap it
fills. NASSCAL treats this field under modern apocrypha. [A59]

## Twentieth century: historical method, discoveries, and global reinterpretation

1906: Albert Schweitzer's critique. The Quest of the Historical Jesus challenged the
tendency to reconstruct Jesus in the image of modern ideals and emphasized his
eschatological setting. The book also remains a history of earlier scholarship. Its critique
applies equally to religious and secular reconstructions when their conclusions reproduce
assumptions they have not tested. [A45]

Early-mid twentieth century: form criticism and the renewed quest. Rudolf
Bultmann and others studied the smaller forms of tradition and their use in early
communities. Ernst Kasemann's 1953 challenge helped renew debate over continuity
between the historical Jesus and the proclaimed Christ. The familiar sequence of "old,"
"new," and "third" quests is a useful teaching framework, but it compresses ongoing
Jewish, Catholic, and other research that never simply stopped. [A48]

1914 in Jehovah's Witness interpretation. Jehovah's Witnesses understand 1914 as the
beginning of Jesus's heavenly kingship and associate Jesus with Michael. This is a specific
doctrinal interpretation of biblical chronology, not a publicly documented earthly
appearance in 1914. It differs from classical Nicene Christology and from the expectations
of many other Christians. [A38]

1931: Faustina Kowalska. Faustina reported a vision of Jesus on 22 February 1931; her
diary and the Divine Mercy image became central to a major Catholic devotion. This is a
well-documented modern reception history. The reported supernatural encounter is a faith
claim whose institutional recognition does not turn it into independent evidence about an
otherwise unknown first-century episode. [A35]

1945: Nag Hammadi. The discovery of Coptic codices in Egypt greatly expanded access
to early Christian and related religious literature, including texts previously known largely
from opponents. Composition dates, translation dates, and the date of the surviving codices
must be kept separate. The records and movements section supplies the individual texts
and explains why "Gnostic" is an imperfect umbrella.

1947 onward: the Dead Sea Scrolls. The discoveries transformed study of the Jewish
scriptural and sectarian environment. Their significance lies in earlier textual witnesses
and historical context. They do not contain a secret biography proving that Jesus was a
Qumran teacher. [A12]

Mid-twentieth century onward: modern Gnostic churches. Organizations such as the
Ecclesia Gnostica explicitly revive Gnostic spiritual interpretation and sacramental
practice. Their self-descriptions are appropriate evidence of what these communities
believe. Modern revival, claimed spiritual continuity, and demonstrated uninterrupted
organizational succession from ancient Valentinians or Sethians are distinct claims. [A58]

1954 onward: Unification traditions. Sun Myung Moon's movement developed a new
account of the work of Jesus and the completion of redemption, identifying Moon with a
messianic role. Its Divine Principle and later institutional history are modern sources for
these beliefs. The movement illustrates how Christology continued to generate new
religious communities, including communities outside historic creedal Christianity. [A60]

1965: Nostra aetate. Vatican II explicitly rejected assigning responsibility for the passion
to all Jews then alive or to Jews today and condemned antisemitism. This matters for Jesus
research because the history of interpretation has repeatedly projected later hostility onto
a Jewish teacher and his Jewish contemporaries. Reading Gospel conflicts in their specific
settings remains essential. [A42]

1970s onward: Jesus within Judaism, society, and empire. Geza Vermes's Jesus the
Jew (1973), E. P. Sanders's Jesus and Judaism (1985), and subsequent work redirected
attention to Jewish practice, eschatology, and historical context. Scholars also studied
economic conditions, resistance to imperial power, gender, and oral tradition. Later
memory research asked how communities preserve and reshape remembered lives. These
approaches differ in method and conclusions; they do not yield a single agreed full
biography. [A02][A48][A51]

1985 onward: the Jesus Seminar. The Seminar organized collective judgments about
sayings and deeds and brought its work to a large public. Its votes reported the assessment
of its participating scholars under particular assumptions. They should not be mistaken for
a measurement of the opinions of all specialists or for an experimental test of a saying.
[A52]

Late twentieth century onward: new global readings. Liberation theology, Black
theology, feminist and womanist interpretation, postcolonial readings, Pentecostal and
charismatic experience, and Asian and African Christologies expanded the questions

readers ask. These are primarily interpretations of Jesus's significance in particular social
worlds. Their moral and theological arguments should be considered on their own terms,
alongside the historical question of which ancient evidence they illuminate.

## 1988-2026: discoveries, controversies, and current evidence

1988-1989: radiocarbon testing of the Turin Shroud. The test and publication
reported a medieval range for the sampled linen. Debate over samples and methods
continued. The scientific details and limits are assessed in the evidence chapter. [A24]

2002 onward: the James ossuary controversy. Questions about the inscription's
authenticity, ownership history, and association with Jesus's family became a major test
case in biblical archaeology. The existence of a real ancient ossuary, the age of its
inscription, and the identity of its owner are separate evidentiary questions. [A27]

2004-2009: excavated contexts. The Siloam pool and Magdala excavations materially
improved understanding of first-century Jerusalem and Galilee. They illuminate settings of
the Gospel traditions, without producing independent records of individual miracles.
[A19][A20]

2012-2016: the Gospel of Jesus's Wife affair. A spectacularly publicized fragment was
eventually widely judged a modern forgery. The episode exposed the limitations of testing
old papyrus without adequately establishing textual and ownership history. [A28]

2020: scientific study of the Nazareth inscription. Isotope analysis associated the
marble with Kos, complicating a once-popular connection to the resurrection story. The
stone's place of manufacture and the location of its original display must still be
distinguished. [A55]

2022: X-ray linen dating proposal. Researchers proposed an older age for a shroud
sample based on cellulose degradation under assumed storage conditions. The claim is
evaluated as a conditional material analysis, alongside the older radiocarbon evidence.
[A26]

2024: Frankfurt inscription and revised Catholic apparition procedures. The
Frankfurt amulet publicly presented in 2024 provides tangible evidence of third-century
devotion. Separately, the Vatican's new norms generally allow a devotional judgment
without asserting certainty that an alleged phenomenon is supernatural. A favorable
ecclesiastical ruling therefore needs to be read according to the precise category used.
[A31][A32]

2025: radiocarbon and handwriting analysis of the Dead Sea Scrolls. A PLOS One
study combined new radiocarbon samples with an AI-based handwriting model called
Enoch and proposed revisions to dates for a number of manuscripts. Its outputs are date

estimates with uncertainty, not verification of the events described in those manuscripts.
Earlier copies of Jewish texts can refine their transmission history while leaving questions
of authorship and prophetic interpretation open. [A44]

2025-2026: renewed debates over old evidence. Recent scholarship continues to
debate Josephus's Testimonium, the extent of early Christian diversity, and how hostile
descriptions of medieval movements should be used. The source register flags current
examples rather than treating every longstanding textbook conclusion as fixed.

April and September 2026: further shroud research. One paper analyzes retained
1988 sample fragments; another investigates linen bleaching through infrared
spectroscopy. These address material questions, not the independent identification of the
cloth's supposed wearer. [A25][A47]

September 2026: Holy Sepulchre research. Sapienza and the custodial institutions
presented the first volume of the Hagia Polis excavation project, covering methods and the
site's long development. The work documents a burial and worship landscape; claims that
archaeology has newly proved the resurrection or identified Jesus's body go beyond the
published project descriptions. [A22][A23]

Present context. Pew Research Center's report published in 2025 estimated
approximately 2.3 billion Christians in 2020. That is a historical population estimate, not a
measurement for October 2026. Jesus also remains central to Islam, influential in other
religions, and the subject of continuing philosophical, artistic, and secular engagement.
The scale of his influence is demonstrable; it is a different question from the truth of each
claim made about him. [A43]

## A practical order for reading the primary evidence

Begin with 1 Thessalonians, Galatians, 1 Corinthians, and Romans to encounter the earliest
surviving substantial Christian evidence. Then read Mark, Matthew, Luke with Acts, and
John, keeping a separate note of where each account adds, changes, omits, or relocates
material. Read Philippians 2, Colossians 1, Hebrews, and Revelation for further portraits of
the cosmic and exalted Christ, while distinguishing disputed authorship and dates.

Next read Josephus, Antiquities 18.63-64 and 20.200; Tacitus, Annals 15.44; and Pliny,
Letters 10.96-97. The source register explains what each actually establishes. Move to the
Didache, 1 Clement, Ignatius, Justin, and Irenaeus to see how claims about Jesus became
liturgy, doctrine, institutional authority, and controversy.

Then read Thomas, Mary, Peter, the Infancy Gospel of Thomas, the Protevangelium of
James, the Apocryphon of John, the Gospel of Truth, Philip, and Judas as separate works.
Their differences are more informative than a single story about "the suppressed gospels."
Consult NASSCAL's individual entries for manuscript lists, editions, and current

bibliographies. Finally, compare Qur'an 3, 4, 5, and 19 with later Islamic interpretation,
and sample the medieval and modern traditions in their own chronological settings. [A01]

For secondary reading, pair historians who differ: Sanders on Jewish context; Meier's A
Marginal Jew for detailed historical argument; Wright on resurrection and eschatology;
Allison on the limits and possibilities of reconstruction; Bauckham on eyewitness tradition;
and scholarship by Karen King, Elaine Pagels, David Brakke, and specialists in apocrypha
on early diversity. These are reading directions, not an assertion that every volume has
been fully reviewed for this dossier. The linked source register identifies the specific
resources actually used.
